The Moon has terrain Earth cannot rehearse

A convolutional autoencoder was given nothing but elevation — no labels, no geology, not even which planet each sample came from. It learned to match terrain across two worlds, and to show where the matching fails.

01The findingwhat the matching reveals

Earth is about half as good a source of lunar terrain as the Moon itself — and almost no source of its ridges and rims

A lunar patch counts as matched when something is as close to it as lunar terrain typically is to lunar terrain in other regions. By that definition the Moon matches its own held-out terrain half the time. Earth, given an equally large pool of patches, matches it 26.8% of the time: 0.54 times as good a source as the Moon itself.

The shortfall is not spread evenly. Earth's usefulness falls steadily as relief rises.

The four regimes emerged from clustering the learned representation, with no human labelling. Their physical properties were measured afterwards, from descriptors the model never saw, and they line up: relief and slope rise steadily across the ramp. Separation is weak by design — these are four stable positions along a continuum, not discrete types.

R2 is the problem. Steep, high-relief ridge and rim terrain makes up an eighth of the lunar south pole. Other lunar regions supply a match for it 18.1% of the time; Earth supplies one 0.3% of the time — fifty times less. This is not R2 simply being unusual, since the Moon still matches it. The Earth sites rugged enough to qualify — the Alps, the Himalaya — are rugged in the wrong way, carved by ice rather than built by impact.

03Artemis IIIcandidate landing regions

The candidate landing regions richest in rim terrain are the hardest to rehearse

Why these six. All six are among the 13 Artemis III candidate landing regions NASA named in 2022, chosen for continuous sunlight through a 6.5-day surface stay, proximity to permanently shadowed regions, safe slopes, communication with Earth and launch-window availability. Four of them — Haworth, Malapert Massif, Nobile Rim and de Gerlache Rim — remain on the nine-region shortlist NASA published in October 2024. I used these six because each is named after a single crater or massif with published coordinates, so it can be placed on the elevation map without guesswork; the shortlisted regions named after plains or unnamed peaks were left out.

The pattern follows the regime result: the more R2 terrain a region contains, the less of it Earth can supply. For comparison, Earth matches 27.3% of the whole south pole on the same measure.

NASA's candidate regions are about 15 km across; each is approximated here by the full USGS/IAU gazetteer disk of its named feature, which contains the region but is larger — for Peak near Shackleton, Shackleton crater itself. Enrichment divides a site's share of matches by its share of the Earth sample, separating genuine similarity from sample size. With six regions this is a visible pattern, not a statistically established correlation. Sources: NASA, October 2024; the 2022 list of 13.

Which landscapes carry the most lunar terrain

The score counts distinct lunar patches a site covers, per thousand of its own patches. 06Marsthe other half of the challenge

The same method applies to Mars

Nothing in the pipeline is specific to the Moon. It takes elevation in, learns terrain fingerprints without labels, and sets the bar for a match from the target world's own terrain. Pointing it at Mars means new input data and a retrain — no new model design and no labels.

The question it would answer is the same: which Martian terrain can Earth's analog sites rehearse, and which can they not? This was not run during the hackathon; it is the next step.

07Limitswhat this does not establish

Scope

  1. Topography only. No geology, composition, regolith mechanics, thermal environment or volatiles. Two landscapes can match in shape and differ in everything that matters for a mission.
  2. Terrain enclosure is not a permanently shadowed region. It measures occlusion in all 16 simulated azimuths under a 1.5° sun, and says nothing about water ice.
  3. Traversal cost is a slope proxy, not a validated rover mobility model.
  4. 27 hand-picked Earth sites. The absence of a match for R2 describes this sample. Intermediate-relief terrain may exist and simply not be sampled here.
  5. Seven known analogs carry the enrichment test, and several background sites are arguably analogs themselves, which makes the contrast conservative.
  6. The match threshold is model-relative, calibrated on held-out lunar terrain rather than against an established scientific criterion.
  7. Coverage is relative, not absolute. The threshold is set so the Moon matches its own terrain half the time, so Earth's figures are only meaningful beside that baseline.
  8. Artemis regions are approximated by the full disk of each named feature, which is larger than NASA's ~15 km candidate region.
  9. A single training run. Sensitivity to the random seed is untested.
  10. Mars was not run. The method transfers directly (section 06), but no Mars results are reported here.

Data. LOLA LDEM 80S at 80 m/px (Barker et al. 2023, PSJ 4:183); ASTER GDEM v3 and NASADEM, NASA LP DAAC; feature coordinates from the USGS/IAU Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature.

Method. Convolutional autoencoder to a 64-dimensional embedding, trained from scratch on balanced lunar and terrestrial batches with dihedral augmentation; k-means regimes; nearest-neighbour matching. No pretrained model and no language model is part of the pipeline. Code and full method: github.com/ReemFaisal1/lunar-terrain-analogs.
